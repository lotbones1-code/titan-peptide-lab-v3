# The Nasal Peptide Protocol Guide

*A research reference for BPC-157, DSIP, Selank, and Oxytocin intranasal formulations*

Published by Titan Peptide Lab — titanpeptidelab.com
For research purposes only. Not for human consumption.

---

## A note before you read

This guide is written for researchers, laboratory personnel, and scientifically
literate readers who want to understand what the published literature actually
says about intranasal peptide delivery. It is not a user manual. It does not
tell you what to put in your body, at what dose, or at what frequency. The
compounds discussed here are sold as research chemicals and are not approved
by the FDA, EMA, or any regulatory body for human therapeutic use.

Where the word "protocol" appears in this document, it refers to protocols
used in published animal or in-vitro studies — not recommendations for human
use. We cite the original studies by PubMed ID so you can read the primary
sources yourself. In a field where almost everything written online is either
marketing or folklore, reading the primary sources is the only honest way to
form an opinion.

We also try to be explicit about what is unknown. For several of these
compounds, the human evidence is thin, the mechanistic data is preliminary,
and the long-term safety data does not exist. A good researcher should
finish this document with a clearer picture of both what is established and
what isn't.

## 1. Why intranasal delivery is interesting

Peptides have two problems as molecules. First, they are large and polar,
which means they cross lipid membranes — including the intestinal wall and
the blood-brain barrier — poorly. Second, they are fragile. Oral peptide
administration is defeated by gastric acid and by peptidases long before
absorption. Subcutaneous injection bypasses the gut but still leaves the
peptide exposed to plasma peptidases and to a blood-brain barrier that was
evolved specifically to keep molecules like these out of the central nervous
system.

Intranasal delivery is interesting for one reason: the olfactory and
trigeminal nerve pathways that traverse the cribriform plate provide a
route from the nasal epithelium into the central nervous system that
circumvents the blood-brain barrier entirely. This was formalized as a
drug-delivery strategy by Illum (PMID 12618035) and extended by Dhuria and
colleagues (PMID 19877171) and Lochhead and Thorne (PMID 22119441). Born et
al. (PMID 11992114) demonstrated, in humans, that neuropeptides
administered intranasally reach cerebrospinal fluid at concentrations
systemic administration cannot achieve.

For peripherally-acting peptides like BPC-157, intranasal delivery is
interesting for a different reason: the nasal mucosa is highly vascularized
and systemic absorption can be rapid and relatively consistent, especially
for small peptides with favorable physicochemical properties. Nasal
administration is not a magic delivery modality — bioavailability varies
substantially by molecule — but for a subset of peptides, it offers
advantages over both oral and injected routes.

## 2. Nasal vs injectable absorption — the mechanistic case

The nasal cavity contains roughly 150 cm² of surface area in adults,
perfused by the sphenopalatine and anterior ethmoidal arteries. Three
absorption routes are relevant.

**The respiratory epithelium** provides the bulk of systemic absorption.
Here, small peptides can cross paracellularly (between cells, through tight
junctions) or transcellularly (through cells, often via endocytosis). Nasal
bioavailability for peptides in this size range typically runs 1% to 30%,
depending on molecular weight, charge, and formulation. Ugwoke et al. (PMID
16182409) reviewed the mucoadhesive strategies used to extend residence
time on the epithelium, which is otherwise limited by mucociliary clearance
to roughly 15-20 minutes.

**The olfactory epithelium** sits in the upper nasal cavity, underneath the
cribriform plate, and occupies about 10 cm² in humans. Olfactory sensory
neurons project directly into the olfactory bulb. Tracer studies in animals
have demonstrated that molecules applied to the olfactory mucosa can reach
the olfactory bulb within minutes, bypassing the blood-brain barrier.
Gänger and Schindowski (PMID 30081536) review the architectural features
that make olfactory delivery feasible — and its substantial limitations.

**The trigeminal pathway** provides a second route to the CNS through
branches of the trigeminal nerve that innervate the respiratory and
olfactory mucosa. Tracer studies indicate trigeminal transport reaches
pons, medulla, and spinal cord in addition to forebrain structures.

Two caveats are essential. First, the fraction of a nasally-administered
dose that reaches the CNS directly is small — typically well under 1% in
animal studies, and Leng and Ludwig (PMID 26049207) argue the fraction may
be lower still in humans than the oxytocin literature often assumes.
Second, a large fraction of any nasal spray is swallowed, drains
posteriorly, or is cleared by cilia within minutes. Formulation, head
position, spray mechanics, and individual anatomy all affect the fraction
that actually reaches the epithelium of interest.

Against injectable administration, intranasal delivery offers: no needles,
no injection-site reactions, no sharps disposal, potential direct CNS
access, and a compliance profile that makes long-duration studies easier
to run. It gives up: precision of dose, predictability of bioavailability,
and — for some molecules — total exposure.

## 3. BPC-157 — body protection compound

BPC-157 is a 15-amino-acid peptide (GEPPPGKPADDAGLV) derived from a
protective gastric protein. It was characterized at the University of
Zagreb in the early 1990s and the Sikiric group remains the primary
mechanistic investigator (PMID 21548867). Independent replications exist
(Chang et al., PMID 21030672; Hsieh et al., PMID 27847966; Gwyer et al.,
PMID 31140046) but the literature is concentrated in a relatively small
number of research groups. This is a real limitation.

### What the animal literature shows

The most consistent findings across published animal work are:

- Accelerated healing of transected or injured tendons, ligaments, and
  muscles (Chang et al., PMID 21030672; Gwyer et al., PMID 31140046)
- Promotion of angiogenesis, mediated at least partially through VEGFR2
  upregulation (Hsieh et al., PMID 27847966; Seiwerth et al., PMID 23782145)
- Gastroprotective effects in multiple ulcer models (Sikiric et al., PMID
  21548867)
- Modulation of the nitric oxide system and effects on vascular tone
- Possible effects on central dopaminergic and serotonergic systems
  (Sikiric et al., PMID 27138887)

Sample-size, effect-size, and mechanistic specificity vary widely across
these studies. The musculoskeletal healing and angiogenesis findings have
the strongest multi-lab support. The gut-brain-axis work is preliminary.

### Why nasal administration

BPC-157 is widely studied in animals by oral, intraperitoneal, and
intramuscular routes — suggesting substantial oral bioavailability that is
unusual for peptides of this size. Nasal administration has not been the
dominant route in published animal work, but is of interest because it
offers rapid systemic absorption without needles and avoids first-pass
metabolism issues that can complicate interpretation of oral dosing. The
small size (~1.4 kDa) and relative stability of BPC-157 make it a
reasonable candidate for nasal formulation.

### What is not established

There are no published randomized human trials of BPC-157. Long-term
safety data in humans does not exist. The compound is not approved for any
indication by any regulatory body. Claims that BPC-157 "heals" any human
condition are not supported by the current evidence base. The animal data
are suggestive; the human data are largely absent.

## 4. DSIP — delta sleep-inducing peptide

DSIP is a nonapeptide (WAGGDASGE) isolated by Schoenenberger and Monnier
in 1977 from rabbit cerebral venous blood during electrically-induced sleep
(PMID 265570). Its name derives from its ability to increase delta-wave
activity on EEG in recipient animals. It is one of the older peptides in
the research literature and one of the more enigmatic.

### What the literature suggests

Graf and Kastin reviewed DSIP in 1984 (PMID 6328357) and catalogued a
surprising breadth of reported effects — sleep modulation, stress
adaptation, thermoregulation, mild analgesia, and modulation of several
neurotransmitter systems. Kovalzon and Strekalova revisited the literature
in 2006 and titled their review "a still unresolved riddle" (PMID
16539664), which is an unusually candid summary for the field.

Human work is small but exists. Schneider-Helmert and Schoenenberger (PMID
6669647) reported psychophysiological effects in humans. Bes and colleagues
(PMID 1299795) ran a double-blind study of DSIP in chronic insomnia
patients and reported modest effects on sleep architecture. Dick et al.
(PMID 6434331) studied DSIP in withdrawal syndromes.

Mechanistically, DSIP has no clearly identified receptor. It modulates
several neurotransmitter systems rather than acting on a single canonical
pathway. Sudakov et al. (PMID 11782798) and Khvatova et al. (PMID
12747940) describe stress-protective and mitochondrial-respiration effects
in rats that may or may not be related to its sleep-modulating action.

### Why nasal administration

DSIP's central sites of action and its peptide structure make it a
candidate for intranasal delivery in the same way oxytocin is — direct CNS
access is theoretically desirable. The compound is small (~850 Da) and
relatively stable for a peptide.

### What is not established

The receptor for DSIP is not identified. The human sleep-modulation
literature is small and the effect sizes are not large. Whether
intranasally-administered DSIP reaches concentrations at CNS targets
sufficient to reproduce the effects seen with intravenous or intracerebral
administration is not established. Long-term safety data in humans does
not exist.

## 5. Selank — tuftsin-derived anxiolytic

Selank is a heptapeptide (TKPRPGP) developed at the Institute of Molecular
Genetics of the Russian Academy of Sciences as a stabilized analog of
tuftsin (a natural immunopeptide). It is registered as an anxiolytic in
Russia but is not approved in North America or the EU.

### What the literature suggests

Kozlovskaya et al. (PMID 14969415) describe Selank's modulation of adaptive
behavior under stress in animal models. Semenova and colleagues (PMID
17523443) reported effects on brain monoamine content consistent with an
anxiolytic-serotonergic profile. Zozulia et al. (PMID 18454096) ran a
clinical study of Selank in generalized anxiety disorder and neurasthenia
and reported efficacy with a favorable side-effect profile versus
benzodiazepine comparators — though the study design and blinding
conventions differ from Western phase-3 standards.

The mechanistic picture that has emerged: Selank affects GABAergic
neurotransmission (Volkova et al., PMID 26941643) and modulates
hippocampal gene expression (Kolomin et al., PMID 20440934). A medvedev et
al. clinical study (PMID 26356526) examined Selank as adjunctive therapy
for anxiety disorders. Uchakina and colleagues (PMID 18577961) describe
immunomodulatory effects consistent with its tuftsin ancestry.

### Why nasal administration

Selank has been used as an intranasal formulation from the start of its
clinical development in Russia. The compound is very small (~750 Da), does
not cross the BBB in appreciable quantities via systemic routes, and shows
faster onset via the nasal route than any alternative route tested. This
is the most established nasal-administration case in this document.

### What is not established

Independent Western replication of Zozulia's anxiolytic findings is
limited. Long-term safety data beyond the Russian clinical experience is
thin. The question of whether Selank's effects persist after discontinuation
(versus being state-dependent) is not fully resolved.

## 6. Oxytocin — the limits of intranasal neuropeptides

Oxytocin is the most-studied intranasal peptide in the scientific
literature by a wide margin, and the oxytocin literature is where the
assumptions behind intranasal CNS delivery have been most rigorously
challenged. Anyone interested in nasal peptides should understand this
debate.

### The optimistic case

Kosfeld et al. (PMID 15931222) reported in Nature in 2005 that intranasal
oxytocin increased trust behavior in a human economic game. Domes et al.
(PMID 17137561), Guastella et al. (PMID 17888411), and many subsequent
studies extended the finding into social cognition, gaze behavior, and
emotional face processing. Striepens et al. (PMID 24310737) provided
direct evidence that intranasally administered oxytocin elevates CSF
concentrations in humans.

### The skeptical case

Leng and Ludwig (PMID 26049207) published a deliberately-provocative
review titled "Intranasal oxytocin: myths and delusions," arguing that
(a) the fraction of a nasal dose reaching central oxytocin targets is far
smaller than the field has assumed, (b) many behavioral effects in the
literature are context- and person-dependent (see Bartz et al., PMID
21696997), and (c) sample sizes in the foundational studies were small
enough that publication bias is a serious concern. Quintana et al. (PMID
32807845) reviewed a decade of oxytocin research and identified specific
methodological improvements the field needed — improvements that the
earlier literature largely lacks.

### What this means for other peptides

The oxytocin debate matters because it is the best-studied case of nasal
peptide CNS delivery and because the skeptical conclusions there should
raise your prior skepticism about less-studied peptides. If the total CSF
penetration of intranasal oxytocin is genuinely under 0.1% of the
administered dose (as Leng and Ludwig suggest), the target concentrations
required at CNS sites of action have to be re-examined for every nasal
neuropeptide. Mens et al. (PMID 6831225) provides pharmacokinetic context
for these calculations.

### What is not established

Whether the behavioral findings in the human oxytocin literature are
replicable at current methodological standards. Whether intranasal
oxytocin delivery is the mechanism responsible for reported behavioral
effects, or whether peripheral oxytocin signaling explains some subset.
Whether any of this generalizes to oxytocin as a research compound in
amounts outside the narrow range studied in academic work.

## 7. Research stacks reported in the literature

"Stacks" — combinations of peptides used together — are dominantly a
community/internet phenomenon rather than a literature-validated practice.
There are very few published studies that examine co-administration of
multiple peptides in the combinations that appear on peptide forums. Any
stack in this section is reported because it appears in academic animal
work, not because it is "recommended."

### BPC-157 + TB-500 (reported in animal musculoskeletal work)

Animal studies have examined BPC-157 and thymosin-beta-4 fragments
sequentially or jointly for tendon and ligament healing. Gwyer et al.
(PMID 31140046) review the musculoskeletal literature that includes some
of this co-administration work.

### Selank + Semax (the Russian nootropic literature)

Both peptides emerged from the same Russian research program. Ashmarin and
Koroleva (PMID 12561369) review the broader "peptide regulation of
homeostasis" framework in which they were developed. They are frequently
studied sequentially rather than simultaneously, and have non-overlapping
mechanistic profiles (Selank: GABAergic/anxiolytic; Semax: ACTH-analog
neurotrophic/nootropic — see Ashmarin et al., PMID 9213433; Shadrina et
al., PMID 11457573).

### DSIP + sleep protocols

DSIP is most interesting in the context of sleep research rather than as
part of a stack. The sleep literature (Bes et al., PMID 1299795) treats
it as a standalone intervention rather than a combination component.

### Important caution

Real multi-peptide interactions are almost entirely uncharacterized. Peptide
combinations can interact pharmacokinetically (competition for peptidases,
shared transport mechanisms) and pharmacodynamically (converging
intracellular pathways, receptor cross-talk). The safety profile of a
single peptide — already preliminary — does not straightforwardly extend to
a combination. Any combination protocol has less supporting evidence than
the individual components.

## 8. A 12-week research stack framework

The framework below is presented as a structure that appears in published
animal protocols — 2-4 weeks of single-compound work followed by a washout
and a subsequent compound. It is not a human dosing recommendation. The
relevance of 12-week framings comes from the fact that most behavioral
animal studies run 4-12 weeks, which provides the longest reasonably
well-characterized windows in the literature.

### Phase 1 (weeks 1-4): single-compound characterization

Studies suggest that animal research protocols beginning with a single
compound generate the cleanest mechanistic data. Gwyer et al. (PMID
31140046) describes BPC-157 musculoskeletal studies typically running 2-4
weeks. Zozulia et al. (PMID 18454096) describes Selank anxiolytic studies
running 14 days. The literature convention is to establish baseline
measurements, run single-compound exposure, then re-measure.

### Phase 2 (weeks 5-6): washout

Washout periods are standard in the research literature to permit return
to baseline before subsequent exposures. The appropriate length depends on
the peptide's half-life and on the persistence of any downstream effects.
For most of the peptides discussed here, 1-2 weeks is conventional in
animal work.

### Phase 3 (weeks 7-10): second compound or combination

If combination exposure is the research question, published animal
protocols typically introduce the second compound after single-compound
baseline is established. This is where the evidence base thins
substantially — published combination studies are a small fraction of
the total literature.

### Phase 4 (weeks 11-12): measurement window

Research protocols conventionally include a dedicated measurement phase
at the end, with behavioral or biochemical endpoints. What you measure
depends on what you are studying.

### What this framework does not tell you

It does not tell you what amount of any peptide to administer to any
specific organism. It does not constitute a human dosing recommendation.
It does not tell you that 12 weeks is the correct duration for any
specific research question. It does not address safety.

## 8b. Practical considerations that shape animal-study protocols

Researchers designing experiments with nasal peptides encounter a set of
practical issues that rarely appear in review articles but dominate the
success or failure of real protocols.

**Spray device consistency.** A 100-microliter metered spray delivers a
different fraction to the olfactory epithelium than a drop formulation
delivered on the back of a tilted-head preparation. Device-to-device
variability and user-to-user technique variability are both substantial,
and blinding study conditions to the administrator can substantially
reduce this source of noise. The literature on nasal drug delivery is
consistent that device characterization is an overlooked variable.

**Volume per nostril.** Nasal capacity is small. Volumes above roughly
150 microliters per nostril tend to drain posteriorly rather than
contacting mucosa. Split-dose administration (half per nostril, brief
interval, second half) is the convention in well-designed animal
protocols and in the rare human intranasal-peptide trials.

**Head position.** For work targeting the olfactory epithelium, head
tilt matters — upper nasal cavity access requires either a supine
"dog-sniff" position or a head-back orientation during and for 30-60
seconds after administration. Several of the null results in the human
oxytocin literature have been critiqued on exactly this point (Quintana
et al., PMID 32807845).

**Baseline nasal conditions.** Rhinitis, septal deviation, prior nasal
surgery, and chronic decongestant use change absorption. Research
protocols conventionally exclude or stratify on these variables.
Ignoring them produces data with effect-size heterogeneity that no
amount of statistical power can rescue.

**Storage practices during a study.** Peptides stored inconsistently
during a multi-week protocol are no longer the same compound at week 4
that they were at week 1. Protocol documentation should include date of
reconstitution, storage temperature, and a sensible expiration policy
rather than the vague "keep refrigerated" seen in supplement-grade
products.

## 9. Timing, circadian considerations, and mucociliary clearance

The pharmacokinetics of nasal administration are dominated by two
timescales. Mucociliary clearance removes material from the nasal cavity
within roughly 15-20 minutes (Ugwoke et al., PMID 16182409). Absorption
across the epithelium peaks within 5-30 minutes for small peptides. The
practical consequence: the interval between administration and the
relevant measurement matters more for nasal peptides than for most other
routes.

Circadian considerations differ by compound.

**DSIP** is studied in the context of sleep and has been examined for
evening administration in sleep studies (Bes et al., PMID 1299795).

**Selank** is reported in the Russian literature as having effects across
the waking hours; the clinical convention has been multiple daily
administrations.

**Oxytocin** behavioral studies have been conducted across a wide range of
administration times and the timing-dependence of the behavioral effects
is itself an unresolved question (Quintana et al., PMID 32807845).

**BPC-157** acts peripherally for most of the reported endpoints and is
less obviously time-of-day sensitive, though circadian variation in
inflammation and healing processes is well-documented in general.

Nasal-cavity conditions matter. Nasal congestion, rhinitis, recent use of
decongestants, and recent cocaine or other vasoconstrictor exposure all
alter nasal blood flow and absorption. Chronic rhinitis changes mucociliary
function. The literature on nasal drug delivery is explicit that inter- and
intra-individual variability is large (Gänger and Schindowski, PMID
30081536).

## 10. Storage, reconstitution, and stability

Peptides are, in general, less stable than small-molecule drugs. The
primary degradation pathways are:

1. **Hydrolysis** of peptide bonds, especially at elevated temperature or
   extreme pH
2. **Oxidation** of sensitive residues (methionine, cysteine, tryptophan)
3. **Aggregation** — formation of dimers, oligomers, and insoluble
   species
4. **Adsorption** to container surfaces, which can be significant at low
   concentrations
5. **Deamidation** of asparagine and glutamine residues

General stability considerations from the peptide-formulation literature:

- **Lyophilized peptides** are generally stable for months to years at -20°C,
  and often for weeks at 4°C. Room temperature stability for lyophilized
  peptides varies widely.
- **Reconstituted peptides in aqueous solution** are substantially less
  stable than lyophilized material. Most aqueous peptide solutions are
  rated for 1-4 weeks at 4°C depending on peptide, buffer, and
  concentration.
- **Freeze-thaw cycling** accelerates degradation for some peptides
  (aggregation-prone sequences in particular).
- **Light exposure** can accelerate oxidation of tryptophan-containing
  sequences.
- **pH** optima vary. Most peptides are more stable at slightly acidic to
  neutral pH than at strongly basic pH.

For nasal sprays specifically: bacteriostatic water or sterile buffered
saline at physiological pH is conventional. Preservatives in commercial
pharmaceutical nasal sprays (benzalkonium chloride is common) may or may
not be appropriate for a given research formulation. The literature on
peptide nasal-spray stability is much smaller than the literature on
injectable peptide stability.

## 10b. A short primer on reading a peptide COA

A certificate of analysis is the single document most researchers should
know how to read critically, and most don't. Here is what a good COA
communicates and how to interpret each element.

**The header** should identify the compound by full name, the sequence,
the lot or batch number, the date of manufacture, and the expected
storage conditions. A COA without a lot number is not a COA — it is a
marketing document.

**The HPLC trace** is a chromatogram showing peaks corresponding to the
compounds in the sample. The target peptide should be the dominant peak.
Impurity peaks should be quantified and, ideally, identified. A "purity
≥99%" claim without a chromatogram is weak evidence because it cannot
be independently verified. A chromatogram with a clean single peak and
small, characterized impurity peaks is strong evidence.

**The mass spectrum** (ESI-MS or MALDI-TOF) shows the molecular mass of
the peptide. It should match the theoretical mass for the specified
sequence within the expected instrument tolerance (roughly ±1 Da for
MALDI-TOF, tighter for high-resolution MS). This is sequence identity
evidence — the HPLC purity number alone cannot rule out that the peptide
is a different peptide of similar chromatographic behavior.

**The water content** (typically reported via Karl Fischer titration)
indicates how much of the lyophilized mass is the peptide itself versus
residual water. This matters when calculating working concentrations
from lyophilized mass.

**Residual solvent data** reports the solvents used in synthesis
(commonly DMF, TFA, acetonitrile). Not all suppliers report this, and
its presence on a COA indicates the supplier is running full analytical
characterization rather than truncated testing.

**Endotoxin results** are reported in endotoxin units per milligram for
peptides intended for injection work. For nasal research, endotoxin is
less critical but still a signal of supply-chain quality.

**Acetate content** matters because peptides containing basic residues
are often supplied as acetate salts. The listed mass of "peptide" on the
label may be salt-inclusive or salt-free, and a good COA specifies
which. For accurate concentration calculations this matters.

A COA that includes all of these elements, for the specific lot you
received, from a supplier who can produce additional documentation on
request, is substantially stronger evidence than any amount of
marketing copy.

## 11. Sourcing — quality markers that actually matter

This section is the most important part of the document for a researcher
actually trying to distinguish signal from noise in the peptide supply
market. Compound identity and purity are the two factors that determine
whether your experimental results mean anything. Most of the variation in
anecdotal peptide outcomes — particularly in online community reports — is
almost certainly explained by variation in what the product actually
contains.

### What to look for

**HPLC purity ≥99%.** High-performance liquid chromatography is the
standard analytical method for peptide purity. "99% purity" on a
certificate of analysis is meaningful only if the COA shows the actual
chromatogram and specifies the column, mobile phase, and detection method.
A COA that states a purity number without a chromatogram is substantially
weaker evidence than one that includes the trace.

**Mass spectrometry confirmation.** ESI-MS or MALDI-TOF confirmation
establishes that the peptide's molecular weight matches the specified
sequence. Purity by HPLC without mass-spec confirmation leaves open the
possibility that the "peptide" is actually a different peptide of similar
chromatographic behavior.

**Lot-matched COAs.** A COA should correspond to the specific lot of the
product you received, not to a historical lot. Generic COAs are a red
flag.

**Endotoxin testing** is relevant for any peptide intended for injection.
For nasal research, it is less critical but still a marker of supply-chain
seriousness.

**Residual solvent data** from the synthesis process is rarely provided
and rarely requested, but its presence on a COA is a strong positive
signal about the supplier.

**Cold-chain shipping** for peptides in aqueous solution is the standard.
Lyophilized peptides can tolerate room temperature in transit for short
periods; aqueous solutions generally cannot.

### Red flags

- Certificates of analysis with no chromatograms
- Suppliers who cannot identify the manufacturer or who appear to be
  reselling from unknown sources
- Prices substantially below the market median (peptide synthesis has
  real costs; dramatic underpricing usually indicates a real problem)
- Absence of any quality statement beyond "99% pure"
- Health-claim language in product descriptions (indicates the supplier
  is positioning as a supplement company, not a research supplier, and
  generally correlates with weaker documentation)
- Product labeling that does not clearly state "for research purposes
  only" or equivalent

### What a good research supplier looks like

Clear chain-of-custody: synthesis source identified, independent testing
available, lot-specific COAs with chromatograms, appropriate storage and
shipping, transparent pricing, and — critically — a company that positions
itself as a laboratory supplier rather than a consumer health brand. The
research-purposes-only framing is a compliance requirement but it also
correlates with serious documentation practices.

## 12. What the literature does not yet answer

An honest summary of what is unsettled:

**BPC-157.** No published human randomized trials. Long-term safety in
humans: unknown. Bioavailability by nasal route in humans: not
characterized in published literature. Whether the animal musculoskeletal
effects generalize to humans at any exposure: unknown.

**DSIP.** Receptor not identified. Total exposure required for the
reported sleep and stress effects: not well characterized in humans. Why
the same compound has such a diverse effect profile in the literature:
unresolved.

**Selank.** Independent Western replication of the Russian clinical
findings: limited. Comparative efficacy versus established anxiolytics in
a modern trial design: not published. Long-term safety beyond Russian
clinical experience: thin.

**Oxytocin.** Whether intranasal administration reaches central targets at
concentrations sufficient to explain behavioral effects: actively debated.
Replicability of foundational behavioral findings at modern
methodological standards: uncertain. Moderator variables (person, context,
baseline oxytocin tone): unclear.

**Nasal delivery in general.** The fraction of an intranasally-administered
dose that reaches specific CNS targets in humans: not well characterized
for most peptides. Inter-individual variability: large, poorly
characterized, and poorly controlled in most of the human literature.

This is the honest state of the evidence. Researchers working with these
compounds should understand that they are working in a preliminary field
and that overconfident claims — in either the marketing literature or the
skeptical literature — are usually wrong.
